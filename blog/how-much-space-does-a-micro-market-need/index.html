Short answer: a footprint as small as 30 x 30 inches

When we walk a building in Sandy, Draper, or Lehi, the first thing a community manager usually asks is "Where would this even go?" The answer is usually somewhere you already have. A Cache market can fit in a footprint as small as 30 x 30 inches, about the space of a corner chair or a small side table. It needs a standard outlet nearby and a Wi-Fi signal for the tap-to-pay kiosk. That is the whole list.

You do not need to give up a leasing office, a storage closet, or a room you could rent. Most of the spots that work best are walls and corners in common areas you already clean, light, and keep open to residents.

The best spots in an apartment building

The right spot is less about square footage and more about who walks past it. These are the places we look at first:

  • Mail or package room. Nearly every resident comes through, often more than once a week. A wall here is often the strongest spot in the building.
  • Clubhouse. A corner or a kitchen counter area works well, especially if the clubhouse stays open late or doubles as a study or work-from-home space.
  • Lobby. In mid-rise buildings, the lobby is the one place everyone crosses on the way in and out.
  • Elevator bank. People wait here with a free minute, which is exactly when a quick grab-and-go purchase happens.
  • Fitness area. Drinks and snacks next to the gym make sense to residents right away.

Why foot traffic beats unit count

It is natural to think a bigger community needs a bigger market, or that a smaller one does not qualify. In practice, what matters most is how many residents pass one fixed point on a normal day. A smaller garden community where everyone picks up mail at a central building can be a better fit than a larger property where residents are spread across separate buildings with no shared path.

That is why our walkthrough focuses on routes, not floor plans. We look at where residents park, where they get packages, where they wait, and which common areas stay open after the leasing office closes. A small market on a busy path does more for residents than a large one tucked in a back room.

What this looks like in Sandy, Draper, Lehi, and Utah County

Utah apartment communities tend to fall into a few layouts, and each one points to a different spot:

Mid-rise and transit-oriented buildings (Sandy, Draper)

Newer buildings near TRAX and FrontRunner stations usually have a single controlled-access lobby, a package room, and an elevator bank. Any of those can work, and the lobby or package room is often the clear winner because every resident passes it.

Garden-style communities (Lehi, Utah County)

Communities with several walk-up buildings usually centralize mail and amenities in a clubhouse. Here the clubhouse, near the mail kiosks or the fitness room, is typically the best spot.

Student housing (near UVU and BYU)

Student communities in Orem and Provo often have study lounges and clubhouses that stay busy late. Late-night access matters, so a spot residents can reach after the office closes is worth prioritizing.

Insurance, notice, and install timing

  • Insurance: we provide a certificate of insurance (COI) for your location before install.
  • Notice period and install timing: agreed with each property and written into a short, one-page letter of intent (LOI).
  • Revenue share: none by default. The market is a free resident amenity.
  • Equipment: stays Cache's. If the market ever leaves, we remove it.

FAQ

How much space does a micro market need?

A Cache micro market can fit in a footprint as small as 30 x 30 inches. Where it goes matters more than how big the spot is.
